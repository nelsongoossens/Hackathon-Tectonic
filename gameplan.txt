### 1. The client model: a picture of the person, not just the account

Two tiers. The **facts tier** is what core banking already has: balances, income, transactions, recurring payments, address, age, products held. The **understanding tier** is what nobody stores today: goals, stated constraints, life situation, habits, hobbies, relationships, and attitudes (how they feel about risk, debt, investing).

Both live in fixed domains such as income and wealth, housing, mobility, household and dependents, recurring commitments, and goals and constraints. Every record shares the same envelope: the claim, its source (bank data, customer said it, or agent inferred it), a confidence score, the evidence behind it (for example, the six vet payments that suggest a pet), and when it was last confirmed. That envelope is what makes it agent-native. An agent can ask "what do we know about this person's housing, how sure are we, and why?" and get a precise answer.

It's also the bitter-lesson answer: a fixed structured envelope with flexible content inside. As models improve, they fill it with richer understanding without anyone redesigning the schema. Constraints that can be made executable are stored twice, once as a goal ("wants to spend under €150/week on groceries") and once as a rule the system can check.

One addition I'd strongly recommend: the customer can see and correct this model on a "what KBC understands about me" screen. It turns a creepy-sounding idea into a trust feature, and it's your GDPR answer.

### 2. The service graph: everything the bank can do, as a map agents can walk

Every service and sub-step KBC offers is a node: buying a home, a mortgage, car insurance, pet insurance, an address change, savings products. Each node carries four things.

**Relevance conditions** come in two kinds. Hard ones are deterministic checks against the facts tier, such as "must be 18+" or "must own a car." Soft ones are signals for agents to reason about, such as life stage, income trajectory, and stated goals. This is where "a 16-year-old won't buy a house" lives.

**Dependencies** say what each node requires, unlocks, or conflicts with. **The procedure** holds the steps, decision points, forms, legal requirements, and money movements. **The degrees of freedom** are the options, like coverage levels, terms, and amounts.

Each node also has guardrails: what an agent may suggest itself versus what must go to a human advisor, such as investment advice that falls under MiFID suitability rules. The graph is curated by KBC's domain experts, not generated. It's the bank's expertise made machine-readable, and it's why agent suggestions are correct rather than merely plausible.

### 3. The agent loop: cheap and deterministic first, smart where it matters

This is your scalability answer for 2.3 million customers. **Deterministic watchers** run on every event for everyone. They check user-set rules, filter eligibility against the service graph, and detect patterns: a category spending shift, a new recurring payment, a salary change, an address change. They're cheap and predictable. An LLM only wakes up when a watcher fires.

**Sensemaking agents** then update the understanding tier: "recurring vet payments, probably has a pet, medium confidence." **Opportunity agents** walk the service graph starting from whatever changed. They produce candidate *moments*: what to say, why, how confident they are, how urgent it is, and what the customer gains.

Your "€2,000 outside fixed costs" example fits here perfectly. The customer says it in plain language, and an agent compiles it into a deterministic rule the watchers run from then on. The LLM acts as a compiler of intent; plain code does the execution.

### 4. The attention gate: knowing when to shut up

This is the heart of the pitch. Every candidate moment competes for a limited per-customer budget, and the default outcome is silence. The gate scores each moment roughly as value to the customer × confidence × urgency, minus the cost of interrupting.

It keeps two ledgers. The **attention budget** is how much interruption this person tolerates, learned from what they engage with, dismiss, ignore, or mute. The **trust budget** grows with moments that turned out genuinely useful and shrinks with irrelevant or salesy ones. Commercial suggestions cost more trust than pure help, so they only get through once trust is high. A useful test to state out loud to judges: would we show this if KBC earned nothing from it?

Every suppressed moment goes to a **silence log** with its reason. That makes restraint visible, auditable, and, crucially, demoable.

### 5. The generative UI: a fixed shell with a living layer

The static part stays where you expect it: balances, accounts, payments. The dynamic part is a stream of moments that agents compose from a library of safe UI components: a one-tap confirm, a multiple choice, a slider, a short chat, or a voice reply (ElevenLabs is a hackathon partner, so use it). Agents choose and fill components rather than writing arbitrary UI, which keeps things consistent, accessible, and secure.

For example: "Your grocery spending is up 30% this month. Is this a new normal, a temporary thing, or should I help you get back under €150?" Every answer writes back into the client model as *declared*, which outranks *inferred*, and updates the attention ledgers. That closes the loop: the more someone interacts, the less the bank has to guess, and the less it needs to ask.

### How this maps to KBC's questions

Fit is 30% of your score, so say this explicitly. Signals come from the facts tier plus what customers declare in moments. Recognition by situation, behaviour, and intent comes from the understanding tier. Automatic adaptation comes from the gate and the generative UI. It works across products and channels because the service graph is product-agnostic, and a moment can land in the app, an advisor's queue, or a voice call. And it reaches millions at once because deterministic filters come first and LLMs only run on flagged cases.

### What to actually build today

Scope hard. Use three contrasting synthetic customers (a 24-year-old in their first job, a couple saving for a house, a retiree) with six months of generated transactions each. Build a service graph slice of three or four branches: budgeting rules, home purchase, car insurance, pet insurance.

Make the demo a time-lapse. Scrub through the months and watch the client model fill in. The agents propose around 20 moments, the gate lets through three, and the silence log shows why the rest stayed quiet. Then dismiss one moment and show the budget adapt. As a closer, feed the same event (a new recurring vet payment) to two customers and show different outcomes because their trust budgets differ. That's your under-three-minute video.

On security, the Aikido audit specifically hunts for IDOR and authorization flaws, and your whole system revolves around a sensitive per-customer model. Scope every read server-side to the authenticated customer, never trust a customer ID sent from the client, and give agents tools already bound to a single customer. Use only synthetic data, per the rules.
