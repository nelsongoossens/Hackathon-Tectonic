KAIROS: THE FULL VISION
A bank that earns the right to speak
KBC challenge, Tectonic Hackathon, 30 September 2026

This document describes the long-term vision behind the proof of concept. The README documents the current implementation, how to run it and its limitations. Earlier project files call the concept "Moments"; Kairos is the current demo name.

The ambition is a bank that understands the person behind the account, notices meaningful changes, and responds at the right moment. More personalization should mean fewer irrelevant interruptions. The relationship improves when the customer can see what the bank understands, correct it, and control when it speaks.

1. THE CLIENT MODEL: A PICTURE OF THE PERSON

The model has two tiers. The facts tier contains information core banking already has: balances, income, transactions, recurring payments, address, age and products held. The understanding tier captures goals, stated constraints, life situation, habits, hobbies, relationships and attitudes toward risk, debt and investing.

Both tiers use domains such as income and wealth, housing, mobility, household and dependents, recurring commitments, and goals and constraints. Every record carries the same envelope: the claim, its source (bank data, customer declaration or agent inference), confidence, supporting evidence, and the dates when it became valid and was last confirmed. An agent can ask what is known about someone's housing, how certain it is, and why, then receive a precise answer.

A fixed structured envelope allows flexible content. Better models can add richer understanding without replacing the entire schema. Where a constraint can be executed, it is represented both as a goal ("keep groceries under EUR 150 per week") and as a deterministic rule.

The customer can inspect the model in a "what the bank understands about me" screen, confirm or reject an inference, and explain a correction. Declared information takes precedence over inference. Evidence of spending must not become a diagnosis or an unsupported sensitive-trait inference. Uncertain beliefs should remain visibly uncertain and should not trigger intrusive suggestions.

Customer visibility and correction are trust features. Production use also requires appropriate consent and lawful processing, retention and deletion controls, access controls, and privacy review; the prototype does not establish regulatory compliance.

2. THE SERVICE GRAPH: A MAP OF WHAT THE BANK CAN DO

Services and their sub-steps are curated nodes: buying a home, a mortgage, car insurance, pet insurance, an address change, savings products and practical budgeting help. Each node contains relevance conditions, dependencies, a procedure, degrees of freedom and guardrails.

Hard relevance conditions are deterministic checks against known facts, such as age and product eligibility. Soft conditions are signals for reasoning, including life stage, income trajectory and stated goals. Dependencies describe what a service requires, unlocks or conflicts with. The procedure captures steps, decision points, forms, legal requirements and money movements. Degrees of freedom describe options such as coverage, terms and amounts.

Guardrails distinguish suggestions an agent may make from situations requiring a human advisor. Investment suitability and other regulated decisions require domain-specific controls beyond a conversational model.

In the full vision, KBC domain experts curate and maintain this graph. The agent explores the bank's explicit expertise rather than inventing products or procedures. The prototype uses a small hand-written slice and demonstrates advisor routing rather than executing banking operations.

3. THE AGENT LOOP: DETERMINISTIC FIRST, REASONING WHERE IT HELPS

The scale ambition is to support more than 2.3 million customers. Cheap deterministic watchers process events, check customer rules and identify changes: a category spending shift, a new recurring payment, a salary change, an address change or a savings milestone. Eligibility checks remove services that cannot apply.

Watcher hits trigger sensemaking. For example, recurring vet payments may support a medium-confidence belief that a customer has a pet. The model records the supporting evidence and preserves uncertainty. Customer corrections constrain subsequent reasoning.

Opportunity reasoning explores relevant branches of the service graph and proposes candidate moments: what to say, why it matters, confidence, urgency and customer benefit. The attention gate then decides whether the candidate deserves an interruption.

Customer intent can become executable without repeatedly consulting an LLM. "Warn me if I spend more than EUR 2,000 a month outside fixed costs" is compiled into a validated JSON rule. Plain code evaluates the rule thereafter. Model output is never executed as code.

Watchers, eligibility checks and delivery decisions remain inspectable. Structured outputs, caching and offline fallbacks make the prototype replayable. A production system would add durable event streams, distributed processing, monitoring and measured budgets for model calls. The prototype's synthetic watcher benchmark is evidence about one layer, not proof of full deployment at bank scale.

4. THE ATTENTION GATE: KNOWING WHEN TO STAY QUIET

Every candidate competes for a limited per-customer attention budget. Silence is a valid outcome. Conceptually, the gate weighs value to the customer, confidence and urgency against interruption cost.

Two ledgers make the relationship explicit. The attention ledger represents how much interruption the customer tolerates, adapting to engagement, dismissal, ignoring and muting. The trust ledger reflects whether earlier moments were useful and relevant. Commercial suggestions require sufficient trust and meaningful customer benefit. The bank's potential revenue is excluded from the customer-value score.

The guiding question is: would this still be useful if the bank earned nothing from it? A weak upsell should not pass merely because it is profitable. A customer-requested alert has a different basis from an unsolicited offer, because the customer explicitly asked for it.

The gate also checks confidence, contradictions, repeated suggestions, muted categories, remaining attention and advisor guardrails. Every suppressed candidate goes to a silence log with a reason. This makes restraint visible and auditable, and allows operators to explain why the bank spoke or stayed quiet.

5. THE ADAPTIVE UI: A FIXED SHELL WITH A LIVING LAYER

Balances, accounts and payments remain in a familiar shell. A dynamic stream of moments is composed from safe UI components: information, one-tap confirmation, choices, sliders and, in the broader vision, short chat or voice replies. Agents select and fill components rather than writing arbitrary UI.

For example: "Your grocery spending has risen this month. Is this a new normal, a temporary change, or should I help you stay under EUR 150 per week?" The response becomes declared information in the client model and can create a deterministic rule. Feedback updates the ledgers, closing the loop between understanding and delivery.

A moment can appear in the app, be read aloud through a voice channel, or be routed to an advisor with its evidence. The prototype includes ElevenLabs text-to-speech with browser speech fallback; voice input and a real outbound calling workflow are future extensions.

The more the customer chooses to clarify, the less the bank needs to infer or ask again. Accessibility, channel preference and control over commercial contact should remain part of the experience.

6. HOW THE VISION ANSWERS KBC'S CHALLENGE

Signals come from bank facts and customer declarations. Recognition by situation, behaviour and intent comes from an evidence-backed understanding tier. Automatic adaptation comes from feedback, the ledgers, the attention gate and adaptive UI. Cross-product support comes from a shared service graph, with delivery through app, voice or an advisor. The approach to scale starts with deterministic filtering and reserves model reasoning for meaningful changes.

The proof of concept makes these ideas inspectable through three contrasting synthetic customers: Emma, a young customer in her first job; Sam and Noor, a couple saving for a home with lower trust after dismissed offers; and Jef, a retiree who prefers voice. A timeline shows beliefs, proposed moments, gate decisions and the silence log. Customers can answer moments, correct beliefs and set rules. A shared puppy storyline demonstrates how similar events can produce different outcomes because the relationships differ.

Security is part of this architecture. Customer reads and actions are scoped server-side to the authenticated customer, staff tools require the staff role, and moment ownership is checked before interactions or voice synthesis. Agents receive customer-scoped context, while structured rules and allowed UI components keep model output from becoming executable code. The demonstration uses synthetic data; API keys and passwords stay outside the repository.

The goal is a stronger customer relationship: useful help when it matters, understandable decisions, customer control, and justified silence the rest of the time.
